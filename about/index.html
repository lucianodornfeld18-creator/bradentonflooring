Why We Exist

Florida breaks floors in predictable ways. We got tired of watching it happen.

Walk through enough Manatee County homes and you start seeing the same casualties over and over: vinyl planks cupping eighteen months after a "professional" install, grout lines cracking along an unlevelled slab, engineered wood delaminating because nobody ever asked what the concrete underneath was doing. None of these failures are mysterious. Nearly all of them trace back to steps that were skipped because they take time, cost a little money up front, and are invisible once the floor is down.

Bradenton Flooring was founded on a simple bet: if the invisible steps are the ones that decide whether a floor lasts, then the invisible steps should be written down, numbered, performed in order and signed off — on every project, whether it's a single guest bathroom in Palmetto or a 3,000-square-foot new build in Lakewood Ranch. That's how the 47-Point Coastal Subfloor & Install Protocol came to be. It isn't marketing language layered onto ordinary work; it's the actual job-site checklist our crews carry, and the homeowner receives the completed copy when we leave.

Three local realities shaped every line of that checklist. First, almost everything here is built on slab-on-grade concrete, and Florida slabs transmit ground moisture upward all year — the leading cause of adhesive failure, cupping and mold under new floors. Second, our subtropical humidity means wood and rigid-core products expand and contract more aggressively than national installation guides assume, so acclimation and expansion gaps can't be eyeballed. Third, thousands of area residents live under HOA and condo association rules that require specific impact-sound ratings before hard flooring is approved — rules that get projects rejected mid-demolition when installers ignore them.

We built a company around solving those three problems correctly, then documented the method so you never have to take our word for it.

The Problems the Protocol Prevents

The five most common floor failures we see on the Gulf Coast

💧 Moisture-driven adhesive failure

Glue-down floors installed over untested slabs release, bubble or breed mold when vapor emissions exceed the adhesive's tolerance. Testing before installation is the only reliable prevention.

📈 Cupping and peaking planks

Products installed straight off the delivery truck haven't equalized with the home's interior climate, so they swell after installation and push against walls with nowhere to go.

🧱 Cracked tile and grout

Large-format tile set over concrete that's out of flat by more than the standard allows will rock, crack and show lippage — a slab problem masquerading as a tile problem.

🔇 HOA rejection after the fact

Condo boards can and do force removal of brand-new floors that were installed without the required acoustic underlayment documentation. That mistake costs twice the original job.

🚪 Failed transitions and trim

Height mismatches at doorways, pinched expansion gaps under baseboards and loose stair nosings are small details that turn into squeaks, buckling and trip hazards within a year.

📄 No paper trail

When there's no record of moisture readings, flatness measurements or product batch numbers, warranty claims stall and repairs become guessing games. We document everything.

The Complete Checklist

All 47 points of the Coastal Subfloor & Install Protocol

This is the working document our installers sign, checkpoint by checkpoint. Five categories, forty-seven items, one completed copy handed to you at the final walkthrough.

Category 1 — Slab Moisture & Flatness11 checkpoints
  • 1. Record slab relative humidity with in-situ RH probes (ASTM F2170)
  • 2. Perform calcium-chloride vapor-emission testing (ASTM F1869) where the adhesive spec requires it
  • 3. Compare all readings against the flooring and adhesive manufacturers' published moisture limits
  • 4. Map slab flatness with a 10-ft straightedge against the 3/16-inch-in-10-ft industry tolerance
  • 5. Mark high spots for grinding and low spots for patch or self-leveler
  • 6. Run a surface-porosity water-drop test to select the correct primer
  • 7. Inspect for cracks, spalling and old cutback adhesive residue
  • 8. Grind ridges and residual thin-set, then HEPA-vacuum the entire slab
  • 9. Apply self-leveling underlayment or patching compound where the slab is out of tolerance
  • 10. Select the vapor barrier or moisture-mitigation system matched to the recorded readings
  • 11. Photograph and log every reading in the project file before installation is authorized
Category 2 — Acoustic & HOA Compliance8 checkpoints
  • 12. Obtain and review the association's flooring addendum and architectural-review requirements
  • 13. Confirm the minimum IIC and STC ratings required for the unit's floor level
  • 14. Specify an underlayment with laboratory-tested ratings that meet or exceed the requirement
  • 15. Collect the manufacturer's acoustic test reports for board submission
  • 16. Prepare and submit the architectural-review packet before any demolition is scheduled
  • 17. Verify certificate-of-insurance requirements with association management
  • 18. Confirm elevator reservations, permitted work hours and common-area protection rules
  • 19. Keep the signed association approval on site for the duration of the installation
Category 3 — Material Acclimation & Layout9 checkpoints
  • 20. Deliver materials 48–72 hours ahead so they acclimate inside the conditioned space per manufacturer spec
  • 21. Verify the home's HVAC is running at normal occupied settings throughout acclimation
  • 22. Check batch and dye-lot numbers across every carton for consistency
  • 23. Open and inspect random cartons for warping, damage and locking-edge defects
  • 24. Measure interior temperature and humidity against the product's installation window
  • 25. Plan plank or tile direction with the homeowner around light sources and sightlines
  • 26. Dry-lay the layout to balance cuts, eliminate sliver pieces at walls and set seam stagger
  • 27. Confirm finished-height changes and transition treatment between adjoining rooms
  • 28. Obtain written homeowner sign-off on layout and pattern before the first row is fixed
Category 4 — Installation & Transitions12 checkpoints
  • 29. Undercut door jambs and casings so flooring slides beneath cleanly
  • 30. Maintain the manufacturer's specified expansion gap at every wall and fixed object
  • 31. Stagger end joints to the product's minimum offset in every row
  • 32. Control tile lippage row-by-row using a leveling-clip system on large formats
  • 33. Verify trowel notch size and adhesive open time against the adhesive data sheet
  • 34. Roll glue-down floors with the specified weighted roller within the working window
  • 35. Confirm locking-edge engagement on every floating-floor row before proceeding
  • 36. Set transition strips at doorways and material changes with flush, secure thresholds
  • 37. Fasten and adhere stair nosings to code with no reliance on adhesive alone
  • 38. Scribe-cut around cabinets, columns and out-of-square walls rather than caulking gaps
  • 39. Reinstall or replace baseboard and quarter-round, then fill and caulk fastener points
  • 40. Protect completed areas from foot traffic and furniture until cure times are met
Category 5 — Final Walkthrough & Care Plan7 checkpoints
  • 41. Inspect every room with the homeowner under both natural and artificial light
  • 42. Test that all transitions, vent registers and trim pieces sit flush and secure
  • 43. Clean the new floor using the manufacturer-approved method and haul away all debris
  • 44. Deliver a written care and maintenance plan specific to the installed product
  • 45. Leave labeled attic stock and record product and lot numbers for future repairs
  • 46. Review the written workmanship warranty and register the manufacturer warranty
  • 47. Homeowner signs the completed 47-point checklist; a copy stays in the permanent job file
